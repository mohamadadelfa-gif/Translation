# Daryabandari Translation-Craft Pilot

## Purpose

This pilot studies **what Najaf Daryabandari does when turning Faulkner into functioning Persian**.

The target is not to imitate the colloquial style of *گور به گور*. The target is to extract reusable translation operations for the Translation Project, especially for making English-source translations read as Persian without losing source meaning, structure, imagery, argument, or voice.

## Evidence base

- William Faulkner, *As I Lay Dying: The Corrected Text*.
- نجف دریابندری، *گور به گور*.
- Twelve aligned cases were selected across distinct narrators and registers.
- The Persian excerpts used in this pilot were checked against the scanned pages for the selected cases.
- Full Persian OCR remains a working transcription; the scan remains authoritative.

## Central finding

Daryabandari's method is best described as:

> **semantic and rhetorical fidelity + syntactic and idiomatic autonomy in Persian**

He preserves what must remain invariant—scene, conceptual relation, image, voice function, repetition, ambiguity, and rhetorical force—while allowing clause boundaries, word order, grammatical packaging, collocations, and register to become genuinely Persian.

This is not "free translation" in the sense of paraphrasing at will. It is **controlled reconstruction**.

# Twelve aligned cases

## 1. DB-001 — Section 1 / Darl

**Source pages:** English PDF p. 2 · Daryabandari PDF p. 6

**Faulkner — short anchor**

> Jewel and I come up from the field, following the path in single file.

**Daryabandari — short anchor**

> من و جوئل داریم از سر زمین برمی‌گردیم، من از جلو او از عقب.

**Operations:** situation-first reconstruction, syntactic unpacking, controlled colloquialization

**What he does:** The participial phrase is not imitated. Daryabandari converts the visual relation into an independent Persian clause. The event is preserved while English grammatical packaging is discarded.

**Rule extracted:** Reconstruct the scene or relation first; then choose the Persian syntax that makes that relation immediately intelligible.

**Transfer to academic translation:** Useful for compressed academic participial phrases: unpack the relation when literal compression produces translationese.

## 2. DB-002 — Section 2 / Cora

**Source pages:** English PDF p. 2 · Daryabandari PDF p. 7

**Faulkner — short anchor**

> The cakes turned out right well.

**Daryabandari — short anchor**

> کیک‌ها خیلی خوب از آب درآمدند.

**Operations:** idiomatic substitution, voice preservation, repetition preservation

**What he does:** A literal equivalent of 'turned out' is avoided. The native Persian resultative idiom از آب درآمدن carries the same everyday evaluative force. Cora's repeated concern with the cakes is kept rather than stylistically cleaned up.

**Rule extracted:** Prefer an established Persian construction that performs the same discourse function over a lexical calque.

**Transfer to academic translation:** For evaluative or resultative academic phrases, search for established Persian collocations before reproducing English verb structure.

## 3. DB-003 — Section 3 / Darl

**Source pages:** English PDF p. 4 · Daryabandari PDF p. 9

**Faulkner — short anchor**

> Warmish-cool, with a faint taste like the hot July wind in cedar trees smells.

**Daryabandari — short anchor**

> آب نیمه‌خنک مزهٔ ملایمی داره، مثل بوی بادی که وسط تابستون از لای درخت‌های کاج بیاد.

**Operations:** sensory image preservation, cross-sensory reconstruction, syntax normalization without flattening

**What he does:** The synesthetic oddity is retained: taste remains compared with smell. Daryabandari makes the relation grammatically Persian without explaining the image away.

**Rule extracted:** When an image is strange because the author intends it to be strange, naturalize the grammar—not the image.

**Transfer to academic translation:** Preserve conceptual metaphors and unusual comparisons in theory/history writing, while allowing Persian syntax to carry them.

## 4. DB-004 — Section 4 / Jewel

**Source pages:** English PDF p. 5 · Daryabandari PDF p. 11

**Faulkner — short anchor**

> Where she's got to see him. Where every breath she draws is full of his knocking and sawing...

**Daryabandari — short anchor**

> درست زیر چشم آدی... هر نفسی که آدی می‌کشه پر از صدای اره و چکش اوست.

**Operations:** fragment restructuring, rhythmic compensation, oral aggression, selective compression

**What he does:** The English fragments are not reproduced mechanically. Their pressure and accusatory rhythm are rebuilt through short Persian units, colloquial syntax, repetition, and forceful verbs.

**Rule extracted:** Preserve the rhetorical pressure of fragmentation even when individual fragment boundaries must change.

**Transfer to academic translation:** In polemical or emphatic prose, preserve argumentative rhythm and repetition, but do not force English fragment syntax onto Persian.

## 5. DB-005 — Section 7 / Dewey Dell

**Source pages:** English PDF p. 9 · Daryabandari PDF p. 17

**Faulkner — short anchor**

> if the sack is full, I cannot help it.

**Daryabandari — short anchor**

> ولی اگه توبره‌ام پر شد دیگه دست خودم نیست.

**Operations:** idiomatic recoding, causal-loop preservation, voice-specific syntax, repetition preservation

**What he does:** The literal phrase 'I cannot help it' becomes the idiomatic Persian دست خودم نیست. More importantly, Daryabandari preserves Dewey Dell's circular self-justification rather than rationalizing it.

**Transfer to academic translation:** When an author deliberately circles, qualifies, or repeats an argument, do not edit away the reasoning pattern merely to make Persian cleaner.

## 7. DB-007 — Section 9 / Anse

**Source pages:** English PDF p. 12 · Daryabandari PDF p. 23

**Faulkner — short anchor**

> when He aims for something to stay put, He makes it up-and-down ways, like a tree or a man.

**Daryabandari — short anchor**

> اگه بخواد یک چیزی سر جاش وایسه سرپا درستش می‌کنه، مثل درخت یا آدم.

**Operations:** conceptual reconstruction, folk-logic preservation, native spatial vocabulary, syntactic simplification

**What he does:** The English opposition longways/up-and-down is rebuilt with Persian embodied expressions such as درازش می‌کنه and سرپا درستش می‌کنه. The absurd folk theology remains intact, but the wording sounds generated inside Persian.

**Rule extracted:** Find the Persian conceptual opposition that lets the same reasoning work, rather than preserving the source's grammatical labels.

**Transfer to academic translation:** Excellent model for conceptual prose: preserve the relation or opposition, but recode it in Persian's own conceptual and syntactic resources.

## 8. DB-008 — Section 11 / Peabody

**Source pages:** English PDF p. 15 · Daryabandari PDF p. 27

**Faulkner — short anchor**

> I believed death to be a phenomenon of the body; now I know it to be merely a function of the mind.

**Daryabandari — short anchor**

> خیال می‌کردم مرگ پدیده‌ای است مربوط به بدن آدم؛ حالا می‌فهمم که فقط یکی از احوال روحی آدمه.

**Operations:** register shift, conceptual paraphrase, terminological naturalization, sentence-level restructuring

**What he does:** The translation rises from colloquial narration into conceptual Persian without becoming a word-for-word philosophical calque. 'function of the mind' is rendered as one of the states/conditions of mind, preserving the thought while choosing more idiomatic Persian abstraction.

**Rule extracted:** Register is local. A translator may move between colloquial and conceptual Persian inside one voice when the source does.

**Transfer to academic translation:** Directly relevant to Zero Hour: use natural Persian conceptual prose, not English-derived noun chains.

## 9. DB-009 — Section 15 / Vardaman

**Source pages:** English PDF p. 22 · Daryabandari PDF p. 39

**Faulkner — short anchor**

> It was not her. I was there, looking. I saw.

**Daryabandari — short anchor**

> خودش نبود. من اونجا بودم. نگاه کردم. دیدم.

**Operations:** radical simplicity preservation, cognitive fragmentation, minimal explicitation, rhythm preservation

# What Daryabandari is actually doing

The cases show that “making a translation Persian” is not one operation. It is a sequence of decisions.

## 1. Separate invariants from variables

### Invariants — normally preserve

- propositional meaning;
- conceptual distinctions;
- causal and logical relations;
- source imagery when meaningful;
- repetition when rhetorically functional;
- ambiguity when intentional;
- narrator/author register;
- degree of certainty, irony, violence, hesitation, or abstraction;
- meaningful formal structures such as Cash's numbered list.

### Variables — may be rebuilt

- clause boundaries;
- sentence length;
- word order;
- grammatical category;
- English participial compression;
- noun-heavy syntax;
- idiomatic packaging;
- explicitness required by Persian;
- connective placement;
- rhythm and pause structure.

A reliable translation can therefore be **far from the source sentence structurally while remaining close to the source functionally**.

## 2. Translate the relation, not the English package

A recurring pattern is:

```text
English construction
        ↓
What relation/event does it encode?
        ↓
How does Persian normally encode that relation?
        ↓
Persian sentence
```

This explains transformations such as:

- participial compression → independent Persian clause;
- English resultative verb → Persian idiom;
- source spatial labels → embodied Persian spatial vocabulary;
- nonstandard English grammar → plausible marked Persian speech.

## 3. Persian verbs do much of the work

Daryabandari often chooses ordinary, powerful Persian verbs instead of abstract nominal equivalents:

- به درد خوردن
- جور درآمدن
- دست خودم نبودن
- از آب درآمدن
- سر جاش وایسادن
- دراز کردن / سرپا کردن

This is especially relevant to academic translation, where English noun phrases often tempt the translator into heavy nominal Persian.

## 4. Register is not global

The translation does not have one permanent “Daryabandari Persian.”

It shifts:

```text
Darl       → visual, lyrical, oral
Jewel      → violent, compressed, breathless
Dewey Dell → circular, impulsive
Tull       → rural, matter-of-fact
Anse       → folk-philosophical
Peabody    → conceptual but still voiced
Vardaman   → childlike fragmentation
Cash       → technical/practical
Addie      → conceptual, intimate, severe
Whitfield  → elevated religious rhetoric
```

Therefore a translation project needs a **register model**, not merely a style guide.

## 5. Naturalization has a boundary

Daryabandari Persianizes **language**, not the fictional world.

He keeps:

- Mississippi;
- the characters;
- material culture;
- source imagery;
- theological and social relations.

He Persianizes:

- syntax;
- idiom;
- rhythm;
- collocation;
- speech register;
- target-language technical vocabulary when a real equivalent exists.

This distinction prevents domestication from becoming distortion.

# Translation-Craft Rules v0.1

These are the first rules that can be transferred into the Translation Mentor.

### TC-01 — Situation before wording
Before translating, state what is happening or what relation the sentence asserts.

### TC-02 — Persian syntax autonomy
Do not preserve English clause structure merely because it is visible.

### TC-03 — Preserve conceptual invariants
Structural freedom is permitted only after meaning, argument, and rhetorical function are identified.

### TC-04 — Prefer native Persian collocations
Search for an established Persian construction before accepting a lexical calque.

### TC-05 — Verbalize when Persian wants a verb
Do not automatically reproduce English nominalization with Persian nouns.

### TC-06 — Preserve meaningful strangeness
Naturalize grammar; do not normalize an intentionally strange image, ambiguity, or cognitive pattern.

### TC-07 — Preserve functional repetition
Do not delete repetition simply because Persian editing convention dislikes repetition.

### TC-08 — Recreate register
Dialect, technical speech, religious rhetoric, conceptual prose, and ordinary narration require different Persian resources.

### TC-09 — Use domain language when it exists
A native technical term is preferable to an improvised descriptive translation when the concepts genuinely match.

### TC-10 — Do not explain what the source leaves unexplained
Clarity added by the translator can destroy ambiguity, voice, or argument.

### TC-11 — Rebuild rhythm
Sentence splitting/joining is allowed when necessary to recreate pace, pressure, hesitation, or calm.

### TC-12 — Back-check after Persianization
After producing natural Persian, compare back against the source invariants to detect lost distinctions.

# Application to the Zero Hour project

Daryabandari's **colloquial forms themselves should not be imported** into *Zero Hour*. What transfers is the method.

For academic prose, the corresponding workflow should be:

```text
SOURCE SENTENCE
      ↓
1. Grammar / argument map
      ↓
2. Identify conceptual invariants
      ↓
3. Identify English-specific packaging
      ↓
4. Retrieve terminology
   Ashouri / Aryanpour / project glossary
      ↓
5. Reconstruct in Persian academic syntax
      ↓
6. Persian-collocation check
      ↓
7. Register check
      ↓
8. Back-check against source
      ↓
9. Mentor diagnosis
```

### Example transformation target

Avoid:

```text
English nominal architecture
→ Persian nominal architecture
```

Prefer:

```text
English argument
→ explicit logical relation
→ Persian verbal/relational syntax
```

But terminology decisions remain controlled by the project glossary. Daryabandari is a **craft model**, not a terminology authority.

# Mentor diagnostic questions

For every difficult draft, the Mentor should ask:

1. What is the sentence actually doing?
2. Which parts are semantic invariants?
3. Which parts are only English grammatical packaging?
4. Does this phrase sound like something written originally in Persian?
5. Is there a Persian collocation or verb that performs this relation better?
6. Have I preserved the source's ambiguity, emphasis, repetition, and register?
7. Have I added an explanation that the source does not contain?
8. Have I preserved a noun-heavy English structure unnecessarily?
9. Does the author still sound like the same kind of speaker/writer?
10. After rewriting for Persian, what information disappeared or was added?

# Pilot conclusion

The most useful lesson is not “translate freely.”

It is:

> **Understand more strictly so that you can translate less literally.**

Daryabandari's freedom is generally downstream of analysis: once the scene, relation, voice, image, or rhetorical effect is understood, he permits Persian to reorganize the surface form.

For the Translation Project, this suggests a dual-control model:

```text
EVIDENCE CONTROL
Ashouri / Aryanpour / source context / glossary
        +
CRAFT CONTROL
Daryabandari-derived Persian reconstruction rules
        ↓
TRANSLATOR DECISION
```

The next research phase should expand this pilot from 12 cases to a larger aligned corpus and test whether these operations recur consistently across the novel.
